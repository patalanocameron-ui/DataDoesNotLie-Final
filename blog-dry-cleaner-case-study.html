The Dry Cleaner That Won Its Customers Back

A multi-store dry cleaner on the edge of closing, a customer file full of people who had quietly left, and the campaigns that brought them back.

The client asked to stay unnamed, so details are anonymized and the figures in these screens are representative.

The situation

A dry cleaner with multiple stores came to us getting ready to close. The counters still looked busy, but the customer file told the real story: the large majority of their base had quietly stopped coming in, and the revenue that remained sat on a small group of regulars. Dry cleaning churn is silent. Nobody cancels, nobody complains, they just do not come back, and by the time it shows up in monthly revenue it looks like a neighborhood problem instead of what it is, a follow-up problem.

They had years of customer and order history sitting in their point of sale. Nobody had ever used it to bring anyone back.

Getting the data in

The shop runs SPOT, the point of sale most dry cleaners know. We connected Daybreak to the SPOT API, and every night it syncs customers, orders, and garment detail on its own. No exports, no retyping, no spreadsheet in the middle. This is the "connect by API" path of Daybreak: if your system has an API, we can wire it in.

The first auto-generated report made the problem impossible to ignore: well over half the base had not visited in six months, and the top 5% of customers carried a huge share of revenue. The report that could have closed the shop became the to-do list instead.

Tagging the base

Averages hide everything in this business, so the first real work was tags. Daybreak segmented the whole customer file into smart lists that keep themselves up to date as behavior changes:

  • VIPs and Regulars, the top 5% and top 20% by lifetime value, the customers who carry the business, worth protecting before anything else.
  • VIP Gone Quiet and Regular Gone Quiet, high-value and mid-value customers who stopped coming. A quiet VIP gets a personal-feeling offer, not a coupon blast.
  • Lapsed 6 Months and Lapsed 12 Months, the win-back lists, split because someone gone a year needs a different message than someone gone a season.
  • Encore 1, 2, and 3, a promo ladder. Whoever the first offer does not bring back moves to the next rung automatically.

The campaigns

With the tags in place, the sends got strategic instead of blast-everything:

  • 3-day sales gave active and cooling customers a reason to come in this week, not someday.
  • Win-back promos went to the lapsed lists by email, with an SMS follow-up for the offer deadline.
  • The Encore ladder escalated automatically: a customer who ignored offer one got offer two a month later, then offer three.
  • Niche offers used the garment data. Customers who bring in Prada and other premium labels got a private premium-care offer instead of a percent-off coupon, because a discount is not what keeps that customer.

The owner's favorite feature

Not the dashboards. The assistant. Daybreak connects to their data over MCP, an open standard for linking AI to software, and the owner asks for what they want in plain English. It comes back built: reports, workflows, smart lists, email templates, SMS snippets, and the kind of insight that would otherwise take an analyst, like which lapsed customers are seasonal and when to catch them.

That is the loop that changed the trajectory: see the problem in the report, ask for the fix in plain English, review it, launch it. The shop stayed open. The lapsed share is shrinking, the offers pay for themselves, and the owner reads one report a month instead of staring at a register screen wondering where everyone went.
